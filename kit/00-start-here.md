# Start here

This chapter tells you which kind of search you are running, what to do in the first
week instead of applying, and how to build a schedule you can actually hold.

## Find yourself in this chapter

### You have unlimited time right now and no structure

If you left your job yesterday, whether that was your choice or not, you have a
problem that looks like an advantage and is not one. A full day with nothing on it
feels like more runway than a search needs, and it is easy to fill that day with
motion, refreshing job boards and rereading the same three listings, without producing
anything that moves the search forward. The fix is not more hours, it is structure,
and this manual gives you that structure starting in this chapter.

### You are still employed and searching in forty minute windows

If you are still working and searching quietly, your constraint is not effort, it is
time and exposure. You get maybe forty minutes on a weeknight after everything else is
done, plus whatever sliver of privacy you can find for a phone screen. The manual is
written to work inside that window as much as it works inside a full open day. Where
the two paths differ, this chapter says so directly.

### The steps are identical for both readers, only pace changes

Every step in this manual applies to both of you. Set up a tracker, write a master
resume, fix your profile, build a schedule, work the schedule. Nothing on that list is
optional for one reader and required for the other. What changes is how much of each
step you can do in a sitting and how many sittings it takes to get through it. A
reader with a full day might finish the master resume in an afternoon. A reader with
forty minutes a night might spend a week on it. Both are on schedule.

## Read the manual in order the first time

### Work the chapters in sequence on your first pass through

The chapters build on each other. The tracker chapter assumes you have not started
applying yet. The resume chapter assumes you have not sent anything out. The outreach
chapter assumes your profile is already fixed. Skipping ahead means doing later work
on top of an unfinished foundation, then redoing it once you go back. Read this manual
start to finish once, even the obvious parts, before treating it as a reference.

### After the first pass, treat the manual as a reference to jump around in

Once you have been through it once, you do not need to reread the whole thing every
week. Go straight to whichever chapter matches whatever is stuck, the interview
chapter before a screen, the outreach chapter when your pipeline is thin, the schedule
section in this chapter when you have fallen off your own plan. The order matters
once. After that, use it like a manual, not like a novel.

## Do not apply to anything in your first week

### Applying in week one spends your best effort on your worst draft

The version of your resume you have sitting on your drive right now is not the one
you should be sending out. It was written for a different job, at a different time,
by a version of you with less clarity about what you are actually looking for now.
Sending it out immediately means your first impression with real employers is made
by unfinished material, and you do not get a second first impression with most of
them. Spend the first week on the material instead of on the applications.

### Set up a way to track the search before you do anything else

Before you write anything or contact anyone, set up a place to record what you apply
to, who you talk to, and what you said you would do next. Without this, a search that
runs more than a few weeks turns into a blur of half remembered conversations and
listings you cannot find again. The chapter on tracking your search covers the
tracker itself in detail. For this week, the only requirement is that it exists before your
first application goes out.

### Write one long master resume before you write a single tailored one

Build a single document that holds everything: every role, every project, every
number you can substantiate, written at full length with nothing trimmed for space.
This is not what you send to anyone. It is the source you cut every tailored version
down from, so that tailoring becomes a subtraction problem instead of a from-scratch
rewrite every time you apply. Writing this once, carefully, in week one saves far more
time across the search than it costs up front.

### Fix your professional profile before a recruiter finds it broken

Your public profile is often the first thing a recruiter or hiring manager sees, and
most people who hire will tell you they look at it before they open a resume, which is
ordinary advice rather than anything anyone has measured for you. Go through it now: current
role, dates, a summary that says what you actually do, and no dead links. If the
platform has a system that helps recruiters find candidates by keyword, that system is
a search and ranking tool a person uses, not an automatic gate that rejects you before
a human looks. Treat it as something to be findable through, not something to trick.

## Build a weekly schedule you can actually hold

### Pick a schedule sized to what you can repeat, not what you can survive once

A schedule that works for one heroic day and collapses on day two is not a schedule,
it is a burst. Write down the actual hours you will spend on the search each day this
week, sized to what you can do again next week and the week after that, for as long as
this search runs. If you are not sure, write down less than you think you can do. A
schedule you keep beats a schedule you admire.

### Set a hard stop and hold it every single day

Decide the time the search work ends each day and stop at that time regardless of
whether you feel finished. A search with no closing time expands to fill every waking
hour, and that is exhausting in a way that makes the whole effort harder to sustain,
not easier. The hard stop is not a suggestion for days when things are going badly. It
is the rule, every day, especially the days when things are going badly.

### A bad week where you did nothing does not undo the plan

At some point you will have a week where you do not follow the schedule at all,
whether from a bad day, a bad string of days, or just running out of the will to keep
going. That week does not mean the plan failed or that you have to start over. It
means you pick the schedule back up at the next scheduled block and keep going. The
plan is built to survive a bad week. Starting over every time one happens is what
actually breaks it.

## If you have a full day, split it into blocks

### A full day of only applications will not fill eight hours

If you try to spend an entire open day just applying to jobs, you will run out of
listings worth applying to long before you run out of hours, and the remaining time
will collapse into refreshing pages that do not change. A full day needs more than one
kind of work in it, or it stops being productive well before it ends.

### Divide the day into searching, writing, outreach, prep, and admin

Break the day into blocks: time spent searching for and evaluating openings, time
spent writing or tailoring material, time spent on outreach to people rather than
postings, time spent preparing for any interview currently on the calendar, and time
spent on the administrative work of updating the tracker and following up. Each of
these uses a different kind of attention, and rotating through them keeps the day from
feeling like the same task repeated until it goes stale.

### Give each block a fixed end time so no single task eats the day

Set a stop time for each block before you start it, the same way you set a hard stop
for the day as a whole. Outreach and writing are the two most likely to expand and
swallow the rest of the schedule if you let them run open ended. When a block's time
is up, move to the next one even if the current task feels unfinished. You can return
to it in tomorrow's block for that kind of work.

## If you have forty minutes, batch by task across the week

### Forty minutes is not enough to run a full daily cycle

Trying to search, write, reach out, and update your tracker every single evening in
forty minutes means doing all four badly instead of one well. A short window works
better when it is dedicated to one kind of task at a time, spread across the days of
the week, rather than compressed into a rushed version of a full day's plan every
night.

### Assign one kind of task to each evening instead of repeating the full cycle nightly

Give Monday to searching and shortlisting, Tuesday to tailoring material for whatever
you shortlisted, Wednesday to outreach messages, and so on through whatever evenings
you actually have available. This turns forty minutes into a real block of focused
work instead of a scattered attempt at everything, and it is easier to hold over
several weeks than a nightly routine that asks for too much every single time.

### Take phone screens away from your employer's building and its network

If a call comes in during your workday, take it somewhere your current employer does
not control, away from the office and off the building's network, ideally from a
parked car or similarly private space you arranged in advance. Treat this as a fixed
logistics rule, not a judgment call to make in the moment when the call is already
ringing.

### Never use an employer laptop, network, or email address for any part of this

Do all search related work, applications, messages, documents, and the tracker
itself, on your own equipment and accounts. This is not about whether your employer
is watching. Employer owned systems are not yours to use for a search for your next
job, and treating that boundary as absolute removes a decision you do not want to be
making under pressure later.

## Measure what you did, not what happened to you

### Track the actions you controlled, not the responses you did not

Judge each week by whether you did the things inside your control, sent the messages
you planned to send, applied to the roles you shortlisted, prepared for the interviews
you had. Do not judge the week by how many replies came back, because that number is
mostly decided by other people on their own schedule and tells you very little about
whether you did your part well.

### The tracking chapter holds the tracker itself, this one only sets up why you need it

This chapter tells you to start tracking before you apply to anything. The chapter
devoted to the tracker covers what to record and how to review it. For now, the only
job is to have a place ready.

## Set your expectations for how long this runs

### Judge the search by the schedule you held, not by the calendar

A search rarely wraps up on the timeline you would pick for it, and there is no
reliable number to give you here, anyone offering you a fixed figure is guessing. What
you can control is whether you held your schedule this week, kept the tracker current,
and kept the material moving forward. Measure the search that way, week by week.
