# Applying

This chapter covers what to send with an application, when a cover letter is worth
the time, and how to reach a human alongside the formal process.

## What to send and where

### Send the tailored cut-down version, never the master document

Send the version you cut down from your master document for this specific listing, not the
master itself. The master is your source material, not something an employer should see. If
you have not built a tailored version yet, that step comes before this one, not during it.

### Apply through the employer's own system where one exists

Where an employer runs its own application system, whether that is a careers page, an
applicant tracking system, or a simple email address it names, use that system rather than
only an aggregator's copy of the listing. That is where the recruiter actually works from,
and the chapter on keywords and applicant tracking systems covers how it works in full. It
is a search and ranking tool for the recruiter, not a gate that auto-rejects you, and
nothing here should be read as implying otherwise.

### Weigh one-click apply on aggregator sites as a real tradeoff

A one-click apply feature on a job board is fast, and it lowers the cost of sending a
marginal application when a listing is a long shot or you are early in a search and still
casting wide. It also tends to leave a thinner record behind, often just a resume with none
of your own material attached, and no tailored answers if the employer later looks for them.
Use it for volume plays and long shots, and use the employer's own system when the role is
one you actually want.

## Answering the application form itself

### Answer disqualifying questions honestly

Questions about work authorization, licenses, location, and shift or travel requirements are
questions the employer configured on purpose, and you can see exactly what they are asking.
Answer them honestly. A false answer here does not disappear, it resurfaces later, usually at
the point where reversing it costs you the most.

### Treat a salary expectation field as a local judgment call, not a script

Some jurisdictions require an employer to publish a pay range, and some restrict what an
employer may ask a candidate directly, and this varies by place and changes over time. Check
what applies where you are before you decide how to answer, rather than following one rule
for every listing. Where a number is genuinely required, give a researched range instead of a
single figure, and be ready to explain how you arrived at it if it comes up later.

### Correct the parsed version of your resume before you submit

Many systems parse your uploaded resume into fields the recruiter will read directly, and
that parse is not always accurate. Look at what the form shows you before you submit, and fix
anything the parser got wrong, a mangled date, a title split across two fields, a skill it
missed, so the version a person reads matches the version you meant to send. The chapter on
applicant tracking systems explains why that parsed record matters. This is the moment you
act on it, and it is the only check in this chapter you cannot do in advance.

## When a cover letter earns its keep, and when it doesn't

### A genuine gap or transition the reader would otherwise have to guess at

If your history includes a gap or a transition that a reader will notice and have to guess
about, a short cover letter lets you say what happened in your own words instead of leaving
the guess to them. This is one of the clearer cases for writing one.

### A change of field the resume alone does not connect

When you are moving into a different field and your resume, on its own, does not make the
connection between what you have done and what the role needs, a cover letter can draw that
line explicitly. Without it, the reader has to do work the resume should have done.

### A referral or a specific connection worth naming

If you have a referral or a specific connection to the employer, naming it plainly in a short
letter is worth the few minutes it takes. This is ordinary practice, not a special tactic, and
most people would tell you to do it.

### An employer that says it reads them

If the employer states that it reads cover letters, or asks a specific question a letter
would answer, take that at face value and write one. Ignoring a stated preference costs you
more than the letter costs to write.

### An employer small enough that a person will actually see it

At a small employer, a real person is more likely to read what you send, all of it, so the
letter has somewhere to land. At a very large employer running a high volume posting where
the upload is marked optional, that is often not true, and this is a genuine disagreement
among people who hire, not a settled rule either way. Skip it there, or write it only if you
have time left over after the resume is properly tailored, since tailoring is where the time
is more likely to matter.

## What a good cover letter contains

### Say why this role at this employer, add one piece of evidence, and stop at a page

A cover letter worth sending says why this role at this employer specifically, not why you
want a job in general, states plainly what you would bring to it, and includes one piece of
real evidence rather than a general claim. Leave out anything that only restates what the
resume already says, keep the whole thing to one page, and hold it to the same honesty rule
as every other document in your search, never claim what you cannot support if asked about it
directly. A cover letter is the document people most often stretch, so be careful here.

## Reaching a human alongside the application

### Send it after the formal application, not instead of it

Reach out to a person only after you have submitted the formal application, never instead of
it, so there is always a record of your application sitting in the employer's own system.

### Name the role, say you applied, and give one specific reason you fit

Identify the likely recruiter or the likely hiring manager for the role, and send a short
message that names the role, says plainly that you have applied, and gives one specific
reason you are a fit for it. Keep it short enough to read comfortably on a phone.

### Ask for a referral only from someone who actually knows your work

A referral from someone already inside the company is the strongest version of this kind of
outreach. Asking for one is a favor with a real cost to the person you ask, so reserve it for
people who genuinely know your work, not a contact you are hoping will vouch for you cold.

## Follow-up, volume, and pace

### Follow up once, after an interval you judge for yourself

Follow up once, after a reasonable interval, and then let it go and put that energy into the
next application instead. There is no fixed number of days that is correct here, the interval
is your own judgment call based on the role and the employer, not a rule this chapter can set
for you.

### Weigh volume against care, and set your own daily cap

A small number of carefully tailored applications and a large number of quick ones are both
strategies people defend, and both can work. The failure mode is not picking either one, it is
doing high volume badly while believing you are still being careful. Cap how many
applications you send in a day so quality does not decay as the day goes on, and pick that
cap yourself based on what you can actually sustain, this chapter cannot set it for you.

### With a whole day free, stop when the quality drops, not at a number

If you have unlimited time today, the risk is grinding out volume long after your attention
has thinned out, producing applications that look tailored but are not. Stop when you notice
the quality dropping, not when you hit some number you set that morning, and put whatever
time is left into the outreach in this chapter instead of another quick application.

### With forty minutes a night, split tailoring and submitting across separate evenings

If you are searching around a job and a phone screen you might have to take from a parked
car, spend one evening tailoring a resume and a different evening submitting it, rather than
doing both in the same tired half hour. Tailoring under time pressure right before submission
is where mistakes get sent. Apply from your own equipment and your own accounts only, never
the employer's laptop, network, or email, and put a personal phone number on the form, since
that is the number that will actually ring.

## Record keeping

### Log what you sent, which resume version, and the date

Keep a record of what you sent, which version of the tailored resume it was, the date, and
the employer's own link to the posting. Weeks later, when a screening call comes in, this is
how you know which version of yourself the interviewer is holding.

### Give out references only when asked, and tell them first

Send references only when a listing or an interviewer actually asks for them, not before, and
tell your references you are giving out their details before you do it, not after.
