# Offers and closing

This chapter covers reading an offer for what it actually contains, the parts of it that are
genuinely negotiable, and how to end a search cleanly whether it finishes in an acceptance or not.

## Reading the offer for what it actually is

### Base pay is the number everything else gets measured against

Base pay is the figure that shows up on every future offer, every raise conversation, and often
every benefit calculation tied to salary, so treat it as the anchor even when a bonus or an equity
number looks larger on the page. Compare it against what you actually need and against what the
market pays for the role, not against your previous salary alone, since the previous number may
itself have been low.

### A bonus is only worth what it has actually paid, not what it could pay

Ask directly whether the bonus is contractual, meaning the offer guarantees it, or discretionary,
meaning the company decides each cycle whether and how much to pay. Ask what it has actually paid
out in each of the last few years, not just the target percentage, since a target is a hope and a
payout history is a fact. A discretionary bonus that paid zero in a bad year is not the same
commitment as a contractual one, and you are allowed to ask the question plainly.

### Equity's value is uncertain and may turn out to be nothing

Find out what kind of equity it is, options, restricted stock units, or something else, and how it
vests, over what period and on what schedule. Do not try to price it yourself, and do not let
anyone price it for you with confidence, because the honest answer, in a private company and often
in a public one tied to future performance, is that the value is uncertain and it may be worth
nothing by the time it vests. Treat any figure attached to it as a possibility, not as compensation
you can already count on.

### Retirement contributions and any employer match are part of the real number

A retirement contribution or match is real, ongoing compensation even though it never touches your
checking account, so include it in the comparison rather than treating it as a footnote. Ask when
it starts, whether it has its own vesting schedule, and whether it applies from your first
paycheck or only after a waiting period.

### Health coverage cost can move the real value of an offer more than base pay does

Ask what the plan actually costs you out of your own pay each month, not just what the company
advertises as covered, and ask about the deductible and the out of pocket maximum if you or anyone
on your coverage uses care regularly. A lower base pay paired with genuinely inexpensive, strong
coverage can beat a higher base pay paired with an expensive plan, and you will not know which is
which until you ask for the actual numbers rather than the summary description.

### Paid time off only matters if the culture actually lets people use it

The number of days on the offer tells you the policy, not the practice. Ask the people you already
spoke with during interviews, not the recruiter, whether the team actually takes its time off or
whether it quietly goes unused. An unlimited policy is not automatically better than a fixed number
of days if nobody on the team feels free to take it.

### The working arrangement is a real term, not a footnote to sort out later

Remote, hybrid, or on site, and the specific number of required days, changes your daily life more
than most of the numbers above, so read it in the offer as carefully as the pay. If a specific
arrangement was agreed verbally during interviews, it needs to appear in writing in the offer
itself, since a verbal understanding that never made it onto paper is not something you can rely on
once you have started.

### Title and level affect the job you can get after this one

Title and level are not vanity. They affect how the next recruiter reads your background, what
roles you are considered for internally, and sometimes what you are paid for years afterward.
Confirm the exact title and level in writing, since a verbal version offered in conversation can
drift by the time the paperwork appears.

### Who you report to and what the job actually is day to day matter most

Neither of these is compensation, and both will shape your daily experience of the job more than
any single line item above. If you have not already met the person you would report to, ask to
before you accept. If the day to day work described in the offer does not match what you understood
during interviews, ask about the gap directly rather than assuming it will resolve on its own once
you start.

## Get the offer in writing before you act on it

### Never resign from anything on a verbal offer

A verbal offer is a stated intention, not a commitment, and things change between a phone call and
a signed letter. Wait for the full written offer before you resign from a current job, turn down
another process, or tell anyone the search is over. If you have already left your last job and are
searching without one, the pull to treat a verbal yes as final is strongest exactly when you can
least afford the mistake, so hold the same rule anyway.

### Read every line of the written offer and ask about anything unclear

Read all of it, not just the pay line, and write down every question before you respond. If you
have a whole day free, do not let this stage consume it in analysis, finish your read, ask your
questions, and keep any other live process moving until you actually sign. If you have forty
minutes an evening and a phone screen you have to take from a parked car, ask for the offer in
writing and then give it a real evening to read rather than answering from your phone in the middle
of a work day.

## What is usually negotiable, and how to ask for it

### Spend your effort on the terms that actually move

What moves varies by employer rather than following a fixed rule, but base pay and a signing amount
are commonly open to discussion, a start date usually has some flexibility, and title or level
occasionally move if you make a clear case. A specific remote or hybrid arrangement sometimes moves
too, but only counts once it is written into the offer itself. Standard benefits and the structure
of equity are usually fixed at the company level and are rarely available to negotiate person by
person, so put your energy into what actually moves.

### Make one consolidated ask, grounded in the role and the market

Gather everything you want changed into a single conversation or message rather than coming back
repeatedly with one more item each time. Ground the ask in the role itself, what the market pays
for it, and what you bring to it, not in your personal expenses, since the employer is buying the
role and not funding your budget. Ask once, clearly, and be ready to accept the answer you get,
including a no. People genuinely disagree about how hard to push here, and the right amount depends
on the employer, the role, and how much you actually want it, so treat this as a judgment call you
are making, not a technique you are applying.

### A real competing offer can be said plainly, an invented one cannot

If you actually have a competing offer, you can say so, plainly and without detail you are not
asked for. Never invent one. An employer can simply accept your stated decision at face value, and
if the offer you claimed does not exist, you have nothing left, the bluff can end the process
outright rather than improve it.

### An employer demanding an instant answer has told you something worth hearing

A company that gives you a few days to read a written offer is behaving normally. One that
pressures you to answer on the spot, or withdraws the offer because you asked a clarifying
question, has shown you in advance how it treats people under pressure, and that is worth weighing
as seriously as anything else in the offer.

## Comparing two offers on more than the headline number

### Compare every component above, then add the manager and the actual work

Lay two offers side by side using every component from the first section, base pay, bonus, equity,
retirement, health coverage, time off, working arrangement, and title and level. Then add the two
things that are not compensation at all, who you would report to and what the job actually is day
to day, since those two often decide more of your daily experience than any number on the page.

## Accepting, telling everyone else, and resigning properly

### Accept in writing, then stop the search rather than let it idle in the background

Once you accept, get that acceptance in writing too, then actually stop, do not let other
applications and conversations keep running quietly while you tell yourself the search is over.
Tell every other live process that you are withdrawing, briefly and politely, since the recruiter
and hiring manager on the other end will remember how you handled it and you may want them again in
a few years.

### Resign in writing, briefly, professionally, and without editorializing

Keep the resignation short and professional regardless of what the job was actually like. Serve the
notice you agreed to and hand your work over properly, because the reference and the reputation you
carry out the door outlast whatever annoyance prompted you to leave.

### Weigh a counteroffer honestly, it solves pay and rarely solves why you left

A counteroffer usually addresses the number that made you unhappy and rarely touches the actual
reason you started looking, and the fact that you were looking is now known regardless of what you
decide. People genuinely disagree about counteroffers in both directions, so weigh this one
honestly against your own reasons for leaving rather than accepting a blanket rule from either side
of the argument.

## References, background checks, and contract terms

### Warn your references before you hand out their details, and expect a check

The applying chapter told you to hand out references only when asked and to warn them first.
This is the stage where that gets tested. Tell each reference the search has reached this point
before you give an employer their contact information, so the call does not surprise them.
Expect a background or employment verification step as a normal part of most hiring processes,
not a sign that anything is wrong.

### Restrictive contract terms vary by jurisdiction, get real legal advice

Non-compete, non-solicit, and intellectual property terms vary enormously depending on where you
live and who you work for, and this manual is not legal advice on any of them. If an offer contains
language that would restrict the work you can do afterward, that is a reasonable moment to get
advice from someone qualified to give it where you actually live, before you sign anything.

## Ending the search cleanly, whichever way it ends

### Close the tracker out and thank the people who took a real risk for you

Close out the tracker rather than abandoning it half updated, and thank the people who referred you
or made an introduction along the way, specifically and to them directly, since that costs you
nothing and they took a real risk in vouching for you. Keep the master document and the tracker
itself rather than deleting them, you will likely need both again, and rebuilding either from
nothing is the expensive part.

### A search that ends without an offer is evidence about fit, not a verdict

If the search ends without an offer, or a process you were sure about does not convert, go back to
the earlier chapters and re-examine the targets and the through line rather than the tactics. Treat
a long search as evidence about fit and about the market you searched in, not as a measurement of
what you are worth.
