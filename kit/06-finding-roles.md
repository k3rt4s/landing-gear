# Finding roles

This chapter covers running a search that does not drift, and checking whether a listing is real
before you spend an hour on it.

## Running the search from your target titles

### Work from the titles you already wrote down, not today's feed

Open the list of target titles you settled on earlier, not the job site's homepage. The feed is
built to show you whatever is newest and most engaging, and it will happily hand you titles you
never chose, adjacent roles, stretch roles, roles in a different function entirely. Search against
your own list first, and treat anything the feed surfaces outside it as a candidate for the ruled
out list, not as a lead to chase on the spot.

### Timebox the search and shortlist, deciding on each listing once

Searching feels like work because it fills time and produces the sensation of progress, but a scroll
session with no shortlist at the end of it produced nothing. Set a fixed block, an hour, ninety
minutes, whatever fits your day, and when it ends, stop searching regardless of how it went. Inside
that block, every listing you touch gets one decision: shortlist it, rule it out, or note it for
later, made once, not reopened every time you pass it again in another tab.

## The three ways a search drifts

### Chasing an adjacent role because a recruiter raised it

A recruiter reaching out about a role near your target, not on it, is common, and their attention
can feel like validation you were not getting from the target search itself. That feeling is not
evidence the adjacent role is right for you. Check it against the ruled out list you wrote earlier.
If the role sits in territory you already decided against, the recruiter's interest does not
change the reasoning that put it there, and the response is still to pass.

### Quietly widening the location or salary floor without deciding to

Drift here rarely looks like a decision. It looks like one listing outside your radius that seemed
worth a look, then another, until the radius has moved without anyone choosing to move it. The same
happens to a salary floor a few thousand at a time. Reread what you wrote down about location and
compensation before the search, out loud if it helps, and treat any listing outside those lines as
a deliberate exception you decide on, not a drift you slide into.

### Applying below your level because it feels like progress

A role a step below where you have been can look like an easy win when the search has gone quiet
for a while, since applying to it feels like doing something rather than nothing. Feeling active is
not the same as making progress toward the role you are actually searching for. Weigh it against
the level you wrote down for yourself, and if it falls short, log it and move on rather than
spending tailoring effort on a role chosen for the wrong reason.

## Verifying a listing before you spend an hour on it

### Confirm the role is still open on the employer's own careers page

Before any tailoring starts, find the listing on the employer's own site, not on the site that
surfaced it to you. Aggregators cache listings and can keep showing a role for days or weeks after
the employer has taken it down. If the employer's own careers page does not show the role, or shows
it as closed, stop there. This is a mechanical check, not a judgment call, and it takes a few
minutes against an hour of tailoring you would otherwise lose.

### Confirm you have the employer's real application link, not a redirect

An aggregator's apply button often routes through one or more intermediary domains before it lands
on an application form. Follow that link and look at where you actually end up. If it deposits you
on the employer's own domain or its named applicant tracking system, you are in the right place. If
it routes through an unrelated domain, or asks you to create an account somewhere with no visible
connection to the employer, treat that as reason to go find the employer's own posting directly
rather than trusting the redirect.

### Confirm it is not a repost of a role you already saw

Reposting is ordinary and does not by itself mean anything is wrong, but it does mean you may be
about to spend effort on a role you already evaluated and passed on, or already applied to. Check
the posting date on the employer's own page, check whether the description matches one you have
read before word for word, and check your own memory and your tracker for whether this employer and
this role already crossed your desk. If it has, treat it as the same lead, not a new one.

## Signals a listing is not what it claims

### No employer named anywhere in the listing

A listing that describes a role in detail but never names who is hiring is worth noticing. Some
legitimate recruiters run confidential searches this way, so it is not proof of anything on its own,
but it removes your ability to do the employer's own careers page check, and that alone is reason to
slow down before investing effort.

### A generic description that appears word for word elsewhere

A description built from interchangeable phrases that would fit almost any employer in the sector,
and that you can find posted verbatim under other company names, was not written about this specific
role. It was written to attract volume. That does not always mean fraud, sometimes it means a low
effort posting from an overworked recruiter, but it means the listing tells you almost nothing real
about the job.

### A link that bounces through several intermediaries before landing anywhere

One redirect is normal. A chain of several, especially one that ends somewhere unrelated to the
employer named in the listing, is not. This is one of the more mechanical signals available to you
and worth checking every time, since it takes seconds and catches a meaningful share of the
listings worth walking away from.

### A request for a date of birth, a national identity number, or bank details

No legitimate employer needs a date of birth, a national identity number, or banking details at the
application stage. Those belong to onboarding, after a written offer, verified through the
employer's own official process. A request for them earlier than that is one of the clearest signals
available, and it does not require any judgment to act on.

### A role continuously posted for months without ever apparently filling

A listing that has been live for months, reposted with the same content and no sign it was ever
filled, may be collecting applicants rather than hiring for a real, currently open position. This
overlaps with the evergreen and pipeline listings covered below, so treat persistence over time as a
reason to spend less effort, not zero effort, unless another signal on this list is also present.

### Contact from a supposed corporate recruiter using a free consumer email domain

A recruiter who claims to represent a company but writes from a free consumer email address, rather
than an address at the employer's own domain, is a mismatch worth noticing on its own. Legitimate
external recruiters exist and often use their own agency's domain rather than the employer's, so
the test is whether the domain matches a real, checkable organization, not whether it matches the
employer by name.

### Any request for payment, equipment purchases, or financial details before a written offer

A legitimate employer does not ask a candidate to pay for training, purchase equipment, or supply
financial account details before a written offer exists. Any version of this request, however the
listing or the recruiter frames it, is reason to stop.

### An interview conducted entirely over a text chat app with no voice or video

A hiring process that stays entirely in a text chat app, never moving to a phone call or a video
call at any point, does not match how real hiring processes for the roles you are targeting usually
run. It is worth noticing on its own even before anything else about the listing looks wrong.

### Do not bother sorting outright fraud from a merely careless listing

Some of the signals above point to a scam and some point to a low quality posting from an overworked
recruiter, and from where you are sitting you often cannot tell which is which. You do not need to.
Both call for the same response, which is to stop before spending effort rather than to investigate
further in order to categorize it correctly first.

## Evergreen and pipeline listings

### Some postings exist to collect applicants, not to fill a role open right now

Some listings are evergreen by design, kept open permanently for high turnover positions, or run as
a pipeline to build a pool of applicants for roles that are not open yet. This is a documented,
ordinary hiring practice, not a trick played on you specifically, and it applies mostly to certain
kinds of roles rather than everything you will see.

### Spend proportionate effort, since you often cannot tell from outside

From the outside, an evergreen or pipeline listing can look identical to a role that is open and
being actively filled right now, and there is no reliable way to tell them apart before you apply.
The practical answer is not to try to detect this perfectly. It is to size your effort to the
listing, spend less time tailoring toward a posting that shows other signs of being evergreen, and
accept that some effort spent this way will not lead anywhere.

## Recording what you find

### Log a lead the moment you decide to pursue it, matched on a stable identifier

Record a lead as soon as you decide to pursue it, not after you have tailored materials for it, and
record the employer's own application link rather than the aggregator's link that first showed it
to you. Job titles get edited, and a reposted role with a new title looks like a brand new lead if
title is what you match on, so match instead on something stable, the employer's own requisition
number or job identifier where one exists. The chapter on tracking your search covers the fields
in full, but the
habit belongs here, at the moment the decision is made.

### Mark a role removed rather than deleting it, and write down what you did not find

When a role closes or disappears, mark it removed or closed in the tracker rather than deleting the
entry, so the history of what you pursued and why survives. And where a field is genuinely unknown,
write that it was not found rather than filling it with a plausible guess, since an unknown and a
confirmed value must never look the same when you read this back in two months.

## Deciding whether a verified listing is worth applying to

### Separate the genuine must-haves from the wish list

Read the requirements honestly once a listing has passed the checks above. Some lines describe what
the role genuinely cannot function without, and some describe a wish list assembled by whoever wrote
the posting. Telling them apart is a real judgment call, and people disagree about where the line
sits on any given listing.

### Treat this as a judgment call, and pace verification to the time you have

Applying only where you match every requirement and applying to everything regardless of fit are
both failure modes, and the right answer sits between them without a formula to compute it. If you
have a full day open, treat searching as one timeboxed block rather than something that runs in the
background all day, and verify listings in a batch once the shortlist is done. If you are searching
around a job in the evenings, give it one or two evenings a week for searching and shortlisting, and
save verification, the more expensive step, for the listings that survive the shortlist rather than
spending it on every lead you notice.
