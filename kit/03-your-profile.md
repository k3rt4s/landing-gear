# Your profile

This chapter covers how a professional profile gets surfaced in a recruiter search, and what to
check in yours line by line.

## Understand what a filter field does before you touch anything

### A recruiter search is a filter, not a scroll through profiles

A recruiter with an open role does not browse profiles the way you might browse a feed. They open
a search tool, set a handful of filters such as title, location, and skills, and then work through
the result set that comes back. If a filter excludes you, the recruiter never sees the rest of your
profile at all, no matter how well it is written. This is the fact that should organize everything
you do to your profile.

### Sort every field on your profile into filter or persuasion before you edit it

Every field on a professional profile does one of two jobs. A filter field decides whether you show
up in a result set at all, things like location, current title, and listed skills. A persuasion
field decides what happens once you are already in front of a reader, things like your summary and
the detail in your experience entries. The two are not interchangeable and they are not worth the
same effort. A beautifully written summary cannot compensate for a location field that keeps you
out of the search in the first place.

### Spend your first evening on filters, your second on persuasion

Work the filter fields first, in one sitting: headline, current position, location, remote
settings, skills, job titles. These are mechanical, checkable, and fast to fix. Come back a
second evening for the persuasion fields: the summary, the experience entries, the photo and
banner. Doing them in the other order means polishing prose that a filtered search will never
reach.

## Fix the headline and the current position field

### Lead the headline with the role you want, not your most recent title

The headline is indexed by the platform's search, and it is also the line that appears next to your
name in a result list, so it is doing double duty as both a filter surface and a first impression.
If your last title was one step behind where you are aiming, a headline that repeats that title
verbatim can keep you out of searches for the role you actually want. Write the headline around the
role you are targeting, using the words a recruiter would type into a search box for that role.

### Fill the current position field even if you have left the job

Many recruiter searches filter on current position, and a profile with that field left blank is
commonly excluded from the result set entirely, not merely ranked lower. If you left your last job,
leaving this field empty is one of the most common and most fixable reasons a qualified person never
appears in a search. Fill it, even though you are not currently employed there.

### Name what you are doing now without implying employment you do not have

You can fill the current position field honestly. Describe what you are actually doing,
consulting, studying, caregiving, or searching, or simply state that you are seeking a role in
your target field. What matters is that the field is not empty, and that the words you put there
do not claim a job you do not have. A line naming your target role and stating that you are
available reads as normal and is not a misrepresentation.

## Set the location and remote fields correctly

### Set your location field to where you can actually be found

Location is a filter field, and it is usually set to the city tied to the platform's own location
data rather than a free text description. Confirm in the platform's own help documentation how that
field is populated and how broadly it matches nearby searches, since this detail differs by platform
and changes without notice.

### Turn on the remote or open to relocate settings that match your search

If you are searching for remote roles, or you are willing to relocate, there is usually a distinct
setting for this, separate from the location field itself, and a recruiter search commonly filters
on it directly. Leaving it at its default can silently remove you from every remote search, even
though your location field looks correct.

### Treat an empty location or remote setting as the most common reason you are invisible

Of every field on a profile, location and its companion remote setting are the ones most likely to
quietly exclude a qualified person from a search they should have appeared in. If you are getting
few or no recruiter contacts and you have not deliberately checked these two fields recently, check
them before you touch anything else.

## Make the skills section an accurate keyword surface

### Build the skills section from the competencies already on your resume

The skills section is indexed and searched directly, so it functions as a keyword surface more than
as a narrative. Populate it from the concrete competencies already listed on your resume rather than
inventing a separate list, so the two documents describe the same person using the same words a
recruiter would search for.

### Remove obsolete skills instead of leaving them to dilute the list

A skills list is more useful short and current than long and stale. A tool or platform you have not
used in years, sitting alongside what you actually do now, does not help you and can crowd out the
terms that matter. Remove it rather than leaving it for completeness.

### Keep job titles conventional and put the creative title in the entry body

Recruiter searches match on conventional job titles far more reliably than on internal branding. If
your actual title at a past employer was something inventive that does not describe the role in
plain terms, keep the conventional equivalent in the title field and put the original internal title
in the body of that entry, where a reader can see it without it costing you a match.

## Write experience and summary as persuasion, not filler

### Give every experience entry enough text to be findable

An entry that lists only a title and a date range contributes almost nothing, either to a search or
to a reader deciding whether you are a fit. Give each entry enough description that its content is
actually searchable and actually informative, not a placeholder waiting to be filled in later.

### Write the first two lines of your summary to stand alone

Most readers see only the opening lines of a summary before deciding whether to expand it, so those
lines need to carry the point on their own. Write the opening as a complete, standalone statement of
what you do and what you are looking for, rather than a sentence that only makes sense once the rest
of the paragraph loads.

### Treat the summary as persuasion, not as another place to stuff keywords

The summary is read by a person who has already found you through the filter fields. Its job is to
make the case for you, not to repeat keywords that belong in the skills section or the headline.
Write it as you would speak to someone who already has your profile open and is deciding whether to
reach out.

## Handle the ordinary polish without overinvesting

### Add a photo, a banner, and a custom address, and know these are ordinary

A profile photo, a banner image, and a custom profile web address are worth having. This is ordinary
advice that most people would give you, not a discovery, and it will not move you from unseen to
seen the way a filter field will. Spend a few minutes on each and move on.

### Do not spend your effort chasing recommendations and endorsements

Recommendations from past colleagues and skill endorsements are low leverage for the work of getting
found and getting contacted. They are pleasant to have but they are not where a limited number of
evening hours should go. If one arrives unprompted, accept it and move on to a field that actually
affects whether you appear in a search.

## Protect a quiet search if you are still employed

### Use the private availability signal built for quiet searching, with its limits

Major platforms commonly offer a setting that signals to recruiters that you are open to
opportunities without displaying that status on your visible profile. Confirm in the platform's own
documentation exactly who this setting shares your status with, since platforms change this behavior
without announcing it, and what looks private in one update can change in the next.

### Turn off change notifications before an editing session

Before you make a pass through your profile, look for a setting that controls whether your network
is notified of profile changes, and turn it off if you are searching quietly. Working through this
chapter naturally touches many fields in a short window, and a burst of change notifications to
current coworkers is the kind of signal a quiet search cannot afford.

### Accept that no setting guarantees your employer will not notice

Be clear with yourself about what these settings can and cannot promise. A private availability
signal reduces the chance a current employer notices you are searching, it does not eliminate it. A
coworker who has your profile open, a search a colleague happens to run, or a platform change you
did not catch can all still surface your activity. Treat every quiet-search setting as a mitigation,
not a guarantee, and plan the rest of your search accordingly, including where you take calls and
when you schedule interviews.

## Confirm the fix with a real search

### Run the search yourself before you trust the summary

Once you have worked through the fields above, run the search a recruiter would actually run. Use
your own target title as the search term, and set the location and remote filters the way a
recruiter hiring for that role would set them. Look at whether your own profile appears in that
result set.

### If you do not appear, fix a filter field, not the prose

If you do not show up, resist the urge to rewrite your summary again. The summary is a persuasion
field, and persuasion only matters once you are in front of someone. An absence from the result set
almost always traces back to a filter field, most often location, current position, or a missing
term in your skills list. Go back to those fields, adjust, and run the search again.
