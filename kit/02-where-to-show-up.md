# Where to show up

This chapter covers which sites are worth a profile, what each one is actually for, and
which ones you can safely skip.

## The two layers of a job search

### Treat the employer's own careers page as the only thing that confirms a role

Everything you will read about in this chapter splits into two layers. The first layer is
discovery: aggregators, job boards, and alerts that surface a lead and tell you a role might
exist. The second layer is confirmation: the employer's own careers page, which is the only
place that tells you whether the role is actually open right now, under the terms the
employer itself is offering. A lead is not a role until you have seen it posted on the
employer's own site. This distinction runs through the rest of the manual, and it applies
to any site this chapter does not mention by name, not just the ones listed here.

### Use aggregators and boards for discovery, not confirmation

Once you accept the two layers, the rest of your setup follows a simple rule. Spend your
discovery time widely, across as many aggregators and boards as you can tolerate, because
discovery is cheap and low risk. Spend your confirmation time narrowly, on the specific
employer's site, before you invest effort in tailoring anything or scheduling anything
around a listing. If a listing cannot be found on the employer's own page, treat that as a
reason to slow down and check, not a reason to assume the aggregator is simply faster.

## The one profile you cannot skip

### Build one strong professional network profile before anything else

Of everything covered in this chapter, one presence is not optional. A single, well kept
profile on a large professional network is the one non-negotiable piece of your setup. This
is documented behavior on that kind of platform, not a guess: recruiters search these
profiles directly, using filters on title, skills, location, and experience, independent of
whatever job postings exist. The next chapter covers what actually belongs in that profile,
field by field. This chapter's job is just to tell you to build it first, before you spread
yourself across boards.

### Understand that recruiters search these profiles directly, not just postings

Because recruiters search profiles as their own dataset, a strong profile can surface you
for a role before it is ever posted publicly, or for a role you would never have found on
your own. That is a different mechanism from a job listing, and it is why this profile
gets priority over every board in the rest of this chapter. Treat filling it out completely
as part of getting set up to search, not as an optional finishing touch you get to later.

## General aggregators and what they are actually good for

### Use aggregators to survey what exists, not to judge whether a role is live

General aggregators pull listings from many sources into one search box, and they are
genuinely useful for surveying a market quickly, seeing what titles are in demand, and
getting a sense of typical requirements for a role you are considering. Use them for that
survey work. Do not use the fact that a listing appears on an aggregator as evidence that
the role is currently open, because the aggregator's job is to collect listings, not to
verify their current status.

### Expect the same role to appear several times through different intermediaries

A single open role can show up on an aggregator more than once, posted by the employer
directly, by a staffing agency working the same requisition, and sometimes by a second
agency working it too. This is an ordinary feature of how postings get syndicated, not a
sign that there are multiple openings. When you see a role duplicated like this, go find the
employer's own listing and apply there if one exists.

### Expect listings to stay up after the role is filled

Aggregators do not always refresh in step with the employer's hiring system, so a listing
can stay visible after the role has already been filled. This is ordinary advice, not a
platform guarantee: check the posting date, and if it looks old, check the employer's
careers page before you spend time on an application for a role that may already be closed.

### Recognize a repost of an old role dressed up as new

A role that did not fill the first time sometimes gets reposted with a fresh date, which can
make a stale opening look brand new on an aggregator. There is no reliable technical way to
detect this from the aggregator alone. If a role's description reads like something you
recall seeing months earlier, that is a judgment call worth trusting, and it is a reason to
read the employer's own page rather than react to the repost date.

## Sector and niche boards

### Find the board that already serves your field before adding another general one

Most sectors have their own job boards that only carry roles in that field, and a niche
board almost always beats another general aggregator, because it filters out the volume of
unrelated listings for you. Non-profit work, public sector roles, academic and research
positions, healthcare, engineering, and the skilled trades each tend to have boards built
specifically for that field. Find the one or two boards that actually serve your sector and
treat them as a standing part of your search, ahead of adding a fourth or fifth general
aggregator to your list.

## Direct signals: employer alerts and public-sector portals

### Set alerts directly on the careers pages of employers you would actually work for

The highest-signal alert you can set is not on an aggregator at all, it is on the careers
page of a specific employer you have already decided you would work for. Most employer
career sites offer some form of notification when new roles are posted, sometimes called
`job alerts` or a similar field on the page. Because this comes straight from the source, it
skips both duplication and staleness. Check that specific employer's own page for how its
alert feature currently works, since the exact mechanism varies by employer and by the
system that page runs on, and it will change over time.

### Learn a public-sector portal's own rules and timeline before you apply through it

Government and public-sector hiring runs through its own portals, on its own rules and its
own timeline, and those timelines are often longer and more procedural than private sector
hiring. Treat a public-sector portal as its own category rather than assuming it behaves
like a general aggregator or a private employer's page. Read that portal's own instructions
for how to apply, how status updates are communicated, and how long a typical process
takes, before you build expectations around it.

## What to skip

### Skip any site that charges you to see or apply to a listing

A legitimate job listing does not require payment from the applicant to view or apply to
it. Any site asking you to pay before you can see a listing's details, or before you can
submit an application, is charging you for something that should be free, and that alone is
reason enough to skip it.

### Skip resume-database sites whose business is reselling your details

Some sites exist mainly to collect resumes into a database and sell access to that database,
rather than to connect you with a specific employer's open role. If a site's value
proposition is unclear beyond an offer to upload your resume and be matched, treat that as
a sign its business model is the database itself, not the match.

### Skip services that offer to apply on your behalf in volume

Services that promise to submit large numbers of applications for you trade quantity for
quality, and a volume of applications you did not read or tailor rarely produces better
outcomes than a smaller number you actually chose. This is an ordinary judgment, not a
platform rule, but it is a consistent one worth applying whenever you see this pitch.

### Skip any site that will not tell you who the employer is

If a listing describes a role without naming the employer, you cannot go confirm it on that
employer's own careers page, which breaks the two-layer model this entire chapter depends
on. Skip listings and sites that will not name the employer, no matter how good the
description reads.

## Searching quietly without exposing yourself

### Use a personal email address and a personal phone number on everything

Use a personal email address and a personal phone number on every profile, application, and
alert you set up, never a work address or a work line. This matters for everyone, but it
matters most if you are searching while still employed, since a work account is the fastest
way for a search to become visible somewhere you did not intend it to be.

### Treat open resume databases as public, because they get scraped

Treat any resume you place in an open, searchable database as effectively public. These
databases get scraped, and the same tools that make them useful to recruiters looking for
candidates are available to any recruiter, including one who works for your current
employer. If you are searching quietly, this is the detail that matters most, and it is
worth checking who can see a given database before you upload anything to it.

### Treat a quiet-search setting as a reduction in risk, never as a guarantee

Some platforms offer a setting that limits who can see that you are actively looking,
sometimes phrased as something like `open to work` visibility controls. This is a real,
documented mechanism on the platforms that offer it, and it is worth turning on if you are
searching quietly. It is not a guarantee. Read that platform's own current documentation for
what the setting actually restricts, and assume it reduces risk rather than eliminates it.

## Keeping this manageable

### Maintain one profile well rather than five stale ones

One profile kept current, complete, and accurate beats five accounts spread thin and left to
go stale. Every additional site you maintain is another place a detail can go out of date,
and an out of date profile can cost you more than the profile you never created in the
first place. When in doubt, put the maintenance effort into the professional network profile
and the one or two sector boards that matter for your field, not into breadth for its own
sake.

### Know how many alerts is too many

Too many alerts stop being signal and start being noise you learn to ignore, which defeats
the purpose of setting them. A small number of well targeted alerts, tied to employers and
sectors you actually care about, will serve you better than a long list you stop reading.
The next chapter that covers running the actual search day to day will come back to how to
work through alerts without letting them drift into a second job of their own.

### Set this up in one sitting only if you plan to wait before judging it

If you have a whole day free, you can set up everything in this chapter in one sitting, and
that is fine. Do not judge the results the same day. Alerts need a few days of real results
before you can tell whether your filters are actually tuned to the roles you want, and
judging them on day one will lead you to tune them against noise.

### Set up one site per evening if that is all the time you have

If you have forty minutes an evening, do not try to set up everything in this chapter at
once. Pick one site per evening, starting with the professional network profile, then the
sector board that fits your field, then the alerts on the employer pages you care about
most. Spread over a handful of evenings, this covers the same ground without turning any one
evening into an overwhelming task on top of everything else already on your plate.
