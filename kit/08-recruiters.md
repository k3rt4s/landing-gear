# Recruiters

This chapter covers the difference between an agency recruiter and an internal one, how to
open a conversation that gets answered, and how to follow up without becoming a problem.

## Who is who and who pays them

### An internal recruiter works for the company and owns the process

An internal recruiter, sometimes called a corporate recruiter, is an employee of the company
doing the hiring. They own the requisition for that specific role, they are the ones who
schedule you, and they are your most direct route into that company's process. When an
internal recruiter reaches out, they are almost always working from a specific opening, not a
general list.

### An agency recruiter is paid by the employer, and is not your agent

An agency recruiter is paid by the employer, not by you, and usually only when a placement
closes. Some agencies work on contingency, meaning several firms may be chasing the same
opening at once and whoever lands a hire first gets paid. Others work retained, meaning one
firm has an exclusive arrangement with the employer for that search. Either way, the agency
recruiter is working their own list of employers and their own quota, not advocating for you
the way a lawyer or an agent would. That is a reason to calibrate what you tell them and how
much you rely on their read of a situation, not a reason to distrust them outright. A good
agency recruiter sees openings, hiring manager preferences, and process quirks that you have
no way to see from outside, and that visibility is genuinely useful when you use it correctly.

## Never let an agency submit you blind

### Never let an agency submit you without naming the employer first

This is the single most important operational rule in dealing with agencies. Before an agency
recruiter sends your resume anywhere, they must tell you the name of the employer. This is not
a courtesy you are asking for, it is protection against a specific and entirely avoidable
failure described in the next check. If a recruiter will not name the employer before asking
you to agree to a submission, that is a clear signal to slow down and ask again, plainly,
before anything moves forward.

### Give permission per employer, never blanket permission

Never give an agency blanket permission to submit you anywhere they see fit. Grant permission
one employer at a time, and only once you know the name. The reason is mechanical rather than
personal. If two different agencies submit the same candidate to the same employer, the
employer now has a duplicate record and an ownership dispute over who gets paid if you are
hired. Employers do not enjoy untangling that, and the simplest resolution from their side is
often to set the candidate aside entirely rather than sort out which agency gets credit. You
avoid that outcome by controlling, one employer at a time, who is allowed to send you forward.

## Opening a conversation that gets answered

### Name the specific role or reason, then give one concrete reason you fit

When you reach out to a recruiter cold, lead with the specific role you are asking about, or
the specific reason you are writing if there is no posted role yet. Follow with one concrete
reason you fit, a piece of relevant experience or a specific qualification, not your whole
background. This is ordinary advice, not a technique, and most people who write good cold
messages are simply doing this rather than something clever.

### Make it short enough to read on a phone and easy to answer

Keep the message short enough that someone can read the whole thing on a phone screen without
scrolling. Do not send a career history or a paragraph-by-paragraph account of your last job.
End with something easy to respond to, a direct question or a clear next step, rather than an
open-ended request that makes the recruiter do the work of figuring out what you want.

## Responding to inbound contact

### Reply even when the role is wrong

When a recruiter contacts you about a role that is not right for you, reply anyway, briefly.
Say plainly what you are actually looking for. That recruiter usually has other openings
beyond the one they wrote to you about, and if they know what you want, they can think of you
later even when today's role is not a fit.

### A short honest no keeps you in mind for the next one

Being unreachable costs you more than being uninterested does. A recruiter who gets silence
from you moves on and forgets you exist. A recruiter who gets a short, clear no along with what
you are actually looking for tends to remember you when something closer to that comes across
their desk. This costs you one short message and nothing else.

## What to ask a recruiter

### Ask whether the role is open and approved right now

Ask whether the role is open and approved, not pending budget or reorganization. A role that is
still waiting on approval can sit indefinitely or disappear, and knowing which kind you are
looking at changes how much hope to invest in it right now.

### Ask whether it is a new role or a backfill, and why the last person left

Ask whether the position is newly created or a backfill for someone who left, and why that
person left if it is a backfill. A pattern of short tenures in the same seat, or a vague answer
about why it is open, tells you something about the role or the team worth weighing.

### Ask who the hiring manager is

Ask who the hiring manager is. Knowing the name, and ideally the title, tells you who actually
owns the decision, and it lets you do a little of your own homework on the team before you
speak with anyone.

### Ask what the stages are and how many rounds to expect

Ask what the stages of the process look like and how many rounds you should expect. A vague
answer here often means the employer has not finalized the process either, which tells you
something about how far along the search really is.

### Ask what the compensation range is

Ask what the compensation range is for the role. A recruiter who gives you a clear range early
is saving both of you time. A recruiter who deflects the question repeatedly is worth noting,
though deflection alone is not automatically a red flag this early.

### Ask what the timeline looks like from here to an offer

Ask what the timeline looks like, from here to an offer if things go well. This does not need
to be precise, but a recruiter with no sense of timeline at all may be working a role that is
not as active as the outreach suggested.

## The compensation question when the recruiter asks first

### Know that disclosure rules vary by place and change, so check locally

Sometimes the recruiter asks you for a number before you ask them for one. What you owe them,
and what they are allowed to ask, depends on where you and the role are located. Some places
require an employer to publish a range up front, and some restrict what an employer may ask a
candidate about pay history or expectations. These rules genuinely vary by jurisdiction and
change over time, so this is a real judgment call rather than one you can resolve with a single
rule from this chapter. Check what applies where you are before you decide how to answer.

### A researched range with a reason behind it beats a single figure

Whatever the local rules require or allow, a researched range with a reason behind it, tied to
the role, the market, and your own experience, serves you better than a single number pulled
from nowhere. A single figure invites a negotiation anchored to that exact point. A range with
reasoning gives you room and gives the recruiter something concrete to work with internally.

## Following up without becoming a problem

### An active process earns a check-in on the agreed timeline

Once you are in an active process, a check-in on whatever timeline was agreed is expected and
normal. If a recruiter told you to expect word by a certain point and that point passes, a
brief message asking for a status update is reasonable and will not read as pushy.

### A cold contact earns exactly one follow-up, not a schedule

A message you sent cold, before any process began, earns exactly one follow-up if you hear
nothing back. There is no fixed number of days that makes this correct, the right interval is a
judgment call based on the situation, but the count is one. After that single follow-up, move
on rather than sending a second and a third.

### Going quiet is usually about their process, not about you

Recruiters go quiet constantly, and it happens for reasons that have nothing to do with you,
budget freezes, a role getting pulled, an internal reorganization, or simply a backlog on their
end. Do not read silence as a verdict on you specifically. The right response is one follow-up,
and then moving your attention elsewhere.

### Batch outreach into blocks, and take calls where you can actually talk

If you have a full day and no structure, batch your recruiter outreach into one block rather
than sending messages all day long, and treat the day's recruiter work as done once that block
ends. If you have forty minutes an evening and a job to protect in the meantime, take calls away
from your employer's building and off its network, arrange a window in advance instead of
answering a call you cannot actually talk on, and let an unexpected call go to voicemail so you
can return it later from somewhere private rather than taking it badly at your desk.

## Keeping the record and staying reachable

### Track which recruiter has you in front of which employer and when

Keep your own record of which recruiter submitted you to which employer, and when. This is the
only real defense against the duplicate submission problem described earlier, since you cannot
avoid a conflict you do not know exists. The chapter on tracking your search covers the tracker
itself in detail.

### Be reachable, and recognize when a contact is not what it claims

Make sure you can actually be reached, a personal phone number with working voicemail, a
personal email address you check regularly, and a realistic answer ready when someone asks
when you can talk. Separately, watch for recruiter contact that is not what it claims to be, a
supposed corporate recruiter writing from a free consumer email address, refusal to name the
employer even after you ask, a request for identity documents or banking details up front, or
pressure to decide immediately. The chapter on finding roles covers this pattern
in full, and the same caution applies here.
