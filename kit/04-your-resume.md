# Your resume

This chapter covers building the long master document you cut every application down
from, and writing achievements instead of responsibilities.

## The master document you build once

### Build one master document you never send to anyone

Before you write a single resume you will actually submit, build one document that holds
every role, every project, every achievement, every tool, and every certification you
have, written out at full length. Nothing gets trimmed in this document. It is not a
resume in the sense of something you send to a person; it is the raw material every
resume you do send gets cut from. Put every job you have held into it, every notable
project inside those jobs, every piece of software or equipment you used well enough to
list, and every certification or credential you hold, current or lapsed.

### Let tailoring become subtraction, not rewriting

The entire reason to build the master document is to change what tailoring feels like.
Without it, every new application means starting from a blank page and trying to
remember what you did five years ago, then wording it under time pressure. With it,
tailoring a resume for one job is a subtraction problem: you already have the material
written, and the work is choosing which parts of it apply here and cutting the rest.
Subtraction is faster and more accurate than reconstruction, and it gets faster every
time you do it because the master keeps growing.

### Keep the master on personal equipment, never an employer's

If you are searching while still employed, keep this document on your own computer and
your own storage, never on an employer laptop, an employer email account, or an
employer-owned cloud drive. This is not a suggestion about tidiness. A document that
lists every achievement at a current employer, stored on that employer's equipment, is a
risk you do not need to carry. Keep it on personal hardware you control.

## Achievements, not responsibilities

### Separate what you were assigned from what changed because you were there

A responsibility describes what you were assigned to do. An achievement describes what
changed because you were the one doing it. "Responsible for customer onboarding" is a
responsibility. "Rebuilt the intake process so new customers stopped waiting on manual
handoffs" is an achievement. Both sentences can describe the same job, but only one of them tells a
reader anything about what you actually produced. Every line in the master document
should eventually become an achievement, even if the first draft of it starts as a
responsibility, because a responsibility is just the raw fact before you have asked what
came of it.

### Ask what would have been different without you

The reliable way to convert a responsibility into an achievement is to ask one question
of it: what would have been different if someone else had done this instead, or if it
had not been done at all. The answer to that question is usually the achievement. If the
honest answer is that nothing would have been different, that line may not belong on the
resume at all. If the answer is that a process would have stayed broken, a deadline would
have slipped, or a team would have kept doing something the slow way, that answer is the
sentence you write down.

### Never claim what you cannot support in an interview

Whatever you write, you have to be able to talk about it in more depth than you wrote it,
because an interviewer will ask you to. This is the working rule to hold every line
against: if someone spent ten minutes asking you follow-up questions about this
achievement, could you answer them without inventing anything on the spot. A plausible
embellishment that falls apart under one follow-up question costs you more than an honest
gap ever would. A visible gap in your history reads as ordinary. A claim you cannot back
up in the room reads as dishonest, and it tends to color everything else you said.

### Describe a change in kind when you have no number for it

Not every achievement comes with a number attached, and you should not invent one to fill
the space. When you have no figure to cite, describe the change in kind instead: what
state the thing was in before, and what state it was in after. "The process ran on a
spreadsheet that had to be rebuilt by hand every week" followed by "the process now runs
without manual rebuilding" is a real achievement with no number in it. That is a stronger
and more honest line than a magnitude you made up to sound more precise than it is.

## The sections a resume needs, in order

### Open with contact details, then a short summary

A resume conventionally opens with your contact details, followed by a short summary of
who you are and what you are looking for. The summary is a handful of sentences, not a
paragraph, and its job is to orient a reader who has never met you, not to restate your
whole history. This is ordinary advice; most people organizing a resume would tell you
the same thing.

### Add a competencies block, then reverse chronological experience

After the summary, most resumes include a short block of competencies or skills, the
tools, methods, and areas you can speak to directly. After that comes the experience
section itself, listing your roles starting with the most recent and working backward.
This is the conventional shape, and it is conventional because it answers the question a
reader has first: what have you done most recently.

### Close with education and certifications

Education and certifications typically close the document. Where you place a
certification within that section depends on how relevant it is to the job at hand; a
credential central to the role you want can be moved earlier if it earns the attention,
but the section itself usually sits last.

### Treat a functional layout as a judgment call, not a shortcut

A functional resume, one organized by skill category instead of by job and date, exists
and some people use it deliberately, often to reduce emphasis on a gap or a scattered
history. This is a genuine judgment call, not a rule, and there is real disagreement
about it. Some readers see a functional layout and simply read the skills. Others read it
and specifically notice that the dates are missing, and read that absence as something
being hidden, whether or not that was the intent. Know that going in, and choose
deliberately rather than by accident.

## Length, dates, and gaps

### Let relevance set the length, not a page count

There is no fixed rule for how long a resume should be, and treat anyone who hands you
one as giving you their own preference rather than a fact. What should actually decide
length is relevance: how much of your history still bears on the job you are applying
for, and how much of it is old enough or far enough afield that it is only diluting the
parts that matter. A resume that includes everything relevant and nothing else is the
right length, whatever that turns out to be.

### State dates plainly, and don't blur a gap with years alone

List the dates on your history plainly and consistently, month and year for each role if
that is your convention, and keep that same convention throughout. Using years alone
instead of months, specifically to make a short gap or a short stint less visible, tends
to be noticed by an experienced reader, and it costs more credibility than the gap itself
would have. A gap stated plainly reads as ordinary. A gap that appears deliberately
obscured reads as something to be suspicious of.

### Leave the reason you left off the resume

Why you left a job, whether it was a layoff, a resignation, or something else, does not
belong on the resume itself. That is a conversation for an interview, if it comes up at
all, not a line item next to a job title.

## Tailoring one master into one application

### Copy the master before you touch anything

Never edit the master document directly when you are tailoring for a specific job. Copy
it first, and do your cutting and rewriting in the copy. The master stays intact as the
full record you draw from next time.

### Cut what isn't relevant, then reorder what's left on top

Working from the copy, cut everything that is not relevant to this particular job, and
reorder what remains so the most relevant work sits at the top of the experience section
rather than buried further down in strict date order. Adjust the summary at the top so it
speaks to this target, in this same pass, rather than leaving a generic summary sitting
above tailored experience underneath it.

### Keep the exact version you sent

Once a version is sent, keep it exactly as sent, saved somewhere you can find it again.
When an interviewer is looking at your resume across the table or the call, you want to
know precisely what they are holding, not a guess at which version it might have been.

## Format, naming, and proofreading

### Save and name the file so it's identifiable out of context

Save the resume in the file format you have been asked for, or a widely readable one if
none was specified, and give the file a name that identifies it on its own once it has
been downloaded and separated from any email or message that explained what it was. A
separate chapter in this manual covers applicant tracking systems in full, including what
they actually do with your file; the short version here is that they are search and
ranking tools a recruiter uses, not an automatic gate, so do not format this resume
around a guess at satisfying one.

### Read it once out loud before you send it

Before you send any version of this resume, read it out loud once, start to finish. This
is ordinary advice, but it catches a category of error that silent reading tends to miss,
the awkward phrase, the repeated word, the sentence that no longer makes sense after
three rounds of editing.

## Pacing the work

### Build the master in one sitting, then rewrite achievements later, if you have the day

If you have a whole day and no other structure pulling at you, use one long block of
that day to build the master document itself, getting every role, project, tool, and
certification down at full length. Leave the achievement rewriting, the responsibility
to achievement conversion covered above, for a second block on another day. That
rewriting is slow work done well, and it does not survive being rushed through at the end
of a long session where your attention is already spent.

### Build the master one job per evening if you have forty minutes a night

If you have forty minutes an evening and a phone screen you have to take from a parked
car, do not try to build the whole master document in one sitting. Build it one job at a
time, one job per evening, and treat the whole document as roughly a week of work rather
than something to finish tonight. Forty minutes is enough to do one job well; it is not
enough to do your whole history well, and trying to rush it defeats the point of building
the master document in the first place.
