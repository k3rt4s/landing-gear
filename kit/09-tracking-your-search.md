# Tracking your search

This chapter covers what to record about each role, why tracking what you did beats tracking
what happens to you, and how to keep that record honest over months of searching.

## Why the tracker exists

### A search that runs more than a few weeks outlives your memory of it

After a handful of weeks you will not remember which version of your resume a particular
recruiter is holding, whether you already applied to a given employer under a different posting,
which recruiter submitted you where, or what you promised to send someone after a call. None of
that is a character flaw. It is simply more detail than a person carries in their head while also
doing the rest of a search. The tracker is the memory the search does not otherwise have.

### Someone will ask you a question only the tracker can answer

A recruiter will ask if you already have an application in with their client. A hiring manager
will ask which round you are actually in. A contact will ask what you sent them last month. These
questions arrive without warning, and answering them wrong costs you credibility at exactly the
moment you need it most. Treat the tracker as required infrastructure for the search, not as
optional bookkeeping you will get to eventually.

## Judge behavior, not outcomes

### What you did is yours, what happens next belongs to someone else

Whether an employer responds, schedules you, or moves you forward is decided by people on their
own timelines, for reasons that are often unrelated to how well you did your part. Mostly, an
outcome does not tell you whether your application was strong or your interview went well. What
you can actually judge is what you did: the roles you shortlisted, the applications you tailored
and sent, the messages you wrote, the interviews you prepared for. Use the tracker to hold that
record, because it is the only part of the process you actually control.

### A week where you did the work and heard nothing is still a good week

Judge each week by whether you did the work, not by whether anyone replied. A week of careful
shortlisting, tailored applications, and real preparation that produced silence is a good week by
this measure, even though it will not feel like one. This is a judgment call about how to stay
sane and effective over a search that may run for months, not a claim that outcomes do not
matter. They matter enormously. They are just not the thing you can move by staring at them, and
they are not the thing a weekly review should be grading.

## What to record for every role

### Employer and role identify what you applied to

Record the employer's name and the title of the role as posted. This sounds obvious, but it is
the anchor everything else in the row hangs off, and it is the first thing you will search for
months later when someone mentions a company name you half remember.

### The employer's own link and requisition id, not the aggregator's

Save the link to the posting on the employer's own careers site rather than the aggregator page
you first saw it on, because aggregator listings expire, get rewritten, or vanish while the
employer's own posting or requisition record often persists. Where the employer assigns a
requisition or job identifier, record that too. It is the one thing a recruiter or an applicant
system will ask for when you follow up, and it survives even if the listing's title changes.

### Date found and date applied anchor the row in time

Record the date you found the posting and, separately, the date you actually applied. The gap
between the two tells you something about your own pace, and both dates matter later when you
are trying to reconstruct how a particular process unfolded or whether you are following up too
soon or too late.

### Resume version and channel show what you sent and how

Record which version of your resume you sent and the channel you applied through, whether that
was the employer's own site, a recruiter, a referral, or somewhere else. Months into a search you
will not remember which resume a given employer is holding, and the channel matters because a
referral or a recruiter relationship changes how and when you should follow up.

### Who you have spoken to

Record the name and role of anyone you have actually spoken with about this opportunity, whether
that is a recruiter, a hiring manager, or a future teammate. This is the row's contact history,
and it is what keeps you from having to ask "have we met" in a second conversation with someone
you already talked to.

### The single next step and its date

Every active row needs exactly one next step and a date attached to it, whether that is
following up, preparing for a scheduled call, or sending something you promised. This field is
what turns the tracker from a list of things that happened into a plan for what happens next.

### Current status

Record where the role actually stands right now, in plain language: applied, screening,
interviewing, offer, closed, or whatever set of stages fits your own search. The status is what
you scan first during a weekly review, so keep it current rather than letting it lag behind
reality.

### Notes

Keep a free-text field for anything that does not fit elsewhere: a detail from a conversation,
something you noticed about the team, a reason you decided to deprioritize a role. This is where
judgment and context live, separate from the structured fields around it.

## Rules that keep the tracker usable over months

### Match a role on something stable, not the title string

The chapter on finding roles told you to log a lead this way at the moment you decide to pursue
it. Here is why the rule exists. Titles get edited, and a role that is reposted with a slightly
different title can look like a brand new opportunity when it is the same job you already applied
to. Match roles on the employer's requisition id where one exists, or on the employer and the
substance of the role together, rather than on the title text alone, or you will end up with
duplicate rows for a single process.

### Keep the fields you write separate from anything imported

If you ever paste in a fresh export or a batch of listings from somewhere else, make sure that
import can never silently overwrite the fields you wrote yourself, your status, your notes, your
next step. A pasted-in refresh that clobbers your own tracking is worse than not importing at
all. If you cannot keep the two kinds of data apart cleanly, do not import in the first place and
add rows by hand instead.

### Mark a role closed rather than deleting the row

When a role is withdrawn, filled, or you decide to stop pursuing it, mark it closed instead of
deleting it. The history of everywhere you applied and what happened is the thing that answers
questions later, both from other people and from yourself, and a deleted row is a question you
will not be able to answer.

### Write not found rather than filling in a plausible guess

When you genuinely do not have a piece of information, write that it was not found instead of
putting in something that looks plausible. An unknown value and a confirmed value must never look
the same when you read the tracker back in two months, because at that point you will trust
whatever is written there, and a guess dressed up as a fact will mislead you at the worst time.

### Give every active row exactly one next step with a date on it

A row with no next step is not actually being worked, whatever its status says. Once a week, look
for rows like this and either give them a next step and a date or close them. A tracker full of
rows with no next step is not a plan, it is a list of things that used to be happening.

## The weekly review

### Read it once a week and ask what moved, what stalled, and what you did

Set aside time once a week to read the whole tracker rather than just adding to it. Ask what
moved since last time, which rows have no next step, which rows have sat untouched long enough to
close, and what you actually did that week regardless of what came back. Also count how many
processes are genuinely live right now, as opposed to the total number of applications you have
ever sent. That live count is usually much smaller than the total, and seeing that gap clearly is
normal and useful, not a sign something is wrong with your search.

## Choosing a tool

### The tool does not matter, and a plain spreadsheet is entirely sufficient

Do not spend time evaluating tools for this. A plain spreadsheet holds everything described in
this chapter, and the specific fields and rules matter far more than the software they live in.
What matters is that it is something you will actually open, that it lives on equipment you
control, and that you can still read it back and make sense of it in six months. Anything meeting
those three requirements is good enough.

## Keeping it honest when things go badly

### The tracker decays exactly when you need it most

The predictable failure mode is that you stop updating the tracker when the search feels like it
is going badly, or you only record the encouraging parts. That is precisely the moment the
tracker is most valuable, because a search that feels like nothing is happening usually looks
different once you can actually see everything you did during that stretch. Update it whether or
not the week went well, especially when it did not.

## Fitting this into the time you have

### With a full day available, update the tracker as part of a short daily admin block

If you have unlimited time, do not leave the tracker to the end of the week. Update it as part of
a short daily admin block instead, because a single day's worth of detail is easy to reconstruct
from memory while a whole week's worth is not. The daily habit also gives a day with a lot of
unstructured time a fixed point of routine.

### With forty minutes an evening, update briefly each session and review once a week

If you are searching quietly around a full time job, update the tracker in a couple of minutes at
the end of each session, and do the fuller review once a week rather than daily. Keep the
tracker, and the search generally, on your own equipment and your own accounts, never on an
employer's laptop, drive, or email, including for a call you have to take from a parked car.
