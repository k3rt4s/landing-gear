# Your story

This chapter covers deciding which roles you are actually going for, and what through line
connects the work you have done to the work you want.

## Stop searching for anything and pick your targets

### Why searching for anything at all produces nothing

If you are open to whatever comes up, you are not searching, you are browsing. A recruiter
typing a title into a search box gets a list of matching people, and if your history reads as
five different jobs with no common thread, you do not surface for any of them. The fix is not
more effort, it is fewer targets. Picking one to three role families is what makes the rest of
this manual possible, because every later chapter, your resume, your outreach, your interview
answers, is built to hit a specific target rather than to be generically acceptable to everyone.

### Choose one to three target role families

Write down the roles you intend to go for, and stop at three. This is an ordinary piece of
advice, most people who have done a search will tell you the same thing, but it is easy to skip
because narrowing feels like giving something up. It is not giving anything up. A fourth target
does not add reach, it dilutes the first three, because your resume and your pitch have to serve
all of them at once and end up serving none of them well. If two of your three targets are close
enough that one resume variant covers both, that is a sign you chose well.

### Write the titles the way a recruiter would type them

For each target, write the title in the plain, conventional form a recruiter would actually type
into a search box, not the title your last company invented for you and not a title you like the
sound of. The systems involved here are recruiter search and ranking tools, not automatic rejection
gates, so there is no keyword score to beat and no machine that rejects a resume before a person
sees it. What they do is let a recruiter search on a title and some skills and get a ranked list
back, and the chapter on keywords and applicant tracking systems covers that in full. If your
headline title does not match the term they typed, you do not appear high in that list, regardless
of how well qualified you are.

### Put your creative title in the body of the entry, not the headline

If your actual title was something invented, a title nobody outside your last company would
recognize, keep it, but move it into the body of the job entry rather than the headline. The
headline carries the conventional title so you surface in search. The invented title still
belongs in the entry itself, next to a plain description of the role, so a reader understands
what you actually did without you pretending the invented title never existed.

## Decide your level honestly

### Name the level you are actually operating at

Decide, in plain terms, whether you are operating at an individual contributor level, a lead
level, a manager level, or a director or above, based on what you actually did day to day, not
on what your business card said. This is a judgment call and people disagree about where the
lines sit, but you have to draw one anyway, because your target titles and your through line both
depend on it. Guessing high to sound impressive and guessing low out of modesty both cost you,
the first invites a screen you cannot pass and the second filters you out of roles you could do.

### When your title was bigger than your actual scope

If your last title outranked what you actually did, say the scope plainly rather than the title.
A title inflated by a reorganization or a generous manager is common, and it is not dishonest to
correct for it, it is dishonest to let the title stand in for the work. Name what you actually
owned, the size of the team or budget or system, and let the target titles you write down reflect
that scope rather than the label you were given.

### When your title was smaller than your actual scope

If you were doing work above your title, the correction runs the other way. Do not undersell
yourself by anchoring your target titles to the label you had. Write down the level the work
actually sat at, and be ready to explain, plainly and without a stated number if you do not have
one, how the scope grew beyond the title. This is the more common failure of the two, because
people default to their old title out of habit rather than reassessing what the job actually was.

## Build the through line as an argument

### State the problem you solve, for whom, and at what scale

A through line is not a story about your career, it is an argument, and an argument has a claim
you can state directly. Name the problem you solve, the kind of organization or team that has
that problem, and the scale at which you have solved it. That is the whole shape. It should hold
across the jobs you have had even if the titles differed, because the through line is about the
function you perform, not the sequence of employers you performed it for.

### Convert responsibilities into achievements

A responsibility says what you were assigned. An achievement says what changed because you were
there. Go through your history and for each responsibility, ask what was different afterward that
would not have been different without you, and write that instead. This is ordinary resume
advice, not a discovery, but it is the single most common gap between a flat resume and one that
argues something, and it is worth doing slowly rather than skimming past it. You need enough of
this now to test whether the through line holds. The resume chapter does the same conversion
properly, line by line across your whole history, so do not try to finish it here.

### Never claim what you cannot support

Treat this as a working rule with a real consequence, not a nicety. Whatever you write into your
through line or your resume, you will be asked to explain in an interview, in more depth than you
wrote it, by someone who has done the work themselves and will notice if you cannot back it up. A
plausible embellishment is worse than a visible gap, because a gap reads as an area you have not
touched yet, and an embellishment you cannot support reads as someone who cannot be trusted on
anything else in the room. Employers verify, sometimes formally and sometimes just by asking a
sharp follow up question, and the claim only has to fail once.

### When you do not have a number, describe the change in kind

If you do not have a figure for an achievement, do not invent one to make it sound more precise
than it is. Describe the change in kind instead: what state the thing was in before, what state it
was in after, and what kind of difference that made to the people who depended on it. A true
description without a number is stronger than a fabricated number, because the true description
survives a follow up question and the fabricated one does not. This rule comes back in the resume
chapter with worked examples, and again in the interview chapter, because it is the one people
break under pressure.

## Test the through line until it holds

### Say it in one sentence

Write the through line as a single sentence you could say to someone who asked what you do. If it
takes you two tries to get through the sentence without backing up to add a clause, it is not
ready yet, and that is expected on a first attempt rather than a sign something is wrong with you.

### Say it in three sentences

Expand the same claim into three sentences that add the problem, the audience, and the scale in
turn, without repeating the first sentence. This version is what you use when someone asks a
genuine follow up question rather than making small talk, and it should still sound like the same
argument as the one sentence version, just with more of it visible.

### Say it in about two minutes

Expand again into roughly two minutes of speaking, enough to cover how you got into this kind of
work, one or two concrete examples of the achievements behind it, and where you want to take it
next. This is the version an interviewer is actually asking for when they open with a general
question about your background, and it should never introduce a new claim that was not implied by
the one sentence version.

### Test it on somebody who does not work in your field

Say the two minute version out loud to someone outside your field and watch whether they can
repeat back what problem you solve. If they cannot, the through line is leaning on jargon or
internal shorthand that only makes sense to people who already know your world, and a recruiter
screening you is often exactly that kind of outside reader. Revise until a plain listener can
follow it, not until it sounds impressive to you.

## When the through line genuinely does not exist yet

### Anchor on the transferable function, not the industry

If you are making a deliberate change in field and the history genuinely does not connect into
one line yet, do not force a connection through the industry, because the industries may
legitimately have nothing to do with each other. Anchor instead on the function you performed,
the kind of problem you solved regardless of what the employer sold, and build the through line
around that function moving into the new field rather than around a continuity of industry that
is not there.

### Expect the first attempt to be too vague, and revise it

The first version of a through line is almost always too vague, a sentence that could describe
many people rather than one. That is a normal first draft, not a sign the exercise failed.
Revise it by cutting any word that could apply to a large number of other people in your field
and replacing it with the specific problem, audience, or scale that is actually true of you.

## Decide what you are ruling out

### Write down the roles you will not go for

Alongside your target titles, write down the roles and titles you are deliberately not going for,
even ones that are tempting. A role that is close to your targets but not on the list is the one
most likely to pull you off course later in the search, when a recruiter reaches out with
something adjacent and it is tempting to say yes just because someone asked. Having the ruled out
list already written means that moment is a decision you already made, not one you have to make
under pressure while a recruiter is on the phone.

### State your reason for leaving plainly, once, without editorializing

Decide now how you will state why you left or are leaving your last role, and state it plainly,
once, without editorializing it every time you repeat it. If it was a layoff, say it was a
layoff. That is a fact about the employer's situation, not a judgment about you, and treating it
as something to soften or explain away only makes it sound like something you believe reflects on
you. Say it the same way every time so it stops costing you energy to repeat.

## Pace this over the first week

### If you have a whole day, do this over two sittings, not one

If you have unlimited time right now, resist doing this chapter in one long sitting. Do a first
pass, then walk away and come back for a second sitting later the same week. The second pass is
where the through line actually gets sharp, because the vague first draft needs distance before
you can see what is generic in it, and a single long sitting tends to lock in the first draft
instead of revising it.

### If you have forty minutes a night, spend your first several evenings here

If you are searching around a job, with forty minutes an evening, spend the first several
evenings on this chapter rather than moving straight to applying. This work is worth more than an
evening spent applying, because every later step, the resume, the outreach, the interview answers,
gets cheaper once your targets and your through line are settled, and every evening spent applying
before that is settled has to be redone once it is.
