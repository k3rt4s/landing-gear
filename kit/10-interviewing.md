# Interviewing

This chapter covers the preparation that carries across every interview, the real examples
worth building once and reusing, and what to do in the days after.

## The preparation that transfers

### Build the reusable core once, because per interview preparation gets thrown away

Most interview preparation is wasted because it is redone from scratch for every interview and
then discarded. Build the reusable core once instead. It has three parts: a set of real examples
drawn from your work history, your through line in a short version for the first thirty seconds
of a screen and a longer version for when someone asks you to walk through your background, and
a standing list of questions you ask in nearly every interview because they are useful in nearly
every job. Once the core exists, a specific interview only adds the research on that employer.

For the reader who has left their job and has a free day or two, build the core in one or two
focused blocks and then keep each interview's specific preparation short. Spending a whole day
preparing for a single screen tends to produce answers that sound rehearsed rather than lived
in. For the reader still employed with forty minutes an evening, build one example per evening
until the core exists, and do the employer specific research the evening before the interview
rather than the hour before it.

### Add only the employer specific research and logistics for each interview

Once the core exists, what a specific interview needs is what the employer does, how it makes
money, anything recent and public about it, and who you are meeting with. This is ordinary
advice, not a special technique, and an hour is usually enough to gather it.

The logistics matter as much as the research. Find somewhere quiet, use headphones, have your
notes and the job posting in front of you, keep water within reach, and make sure your phone is
charged. Never take the call while driving. If you are still employed and searching quietly,
arrange the window in advance, take the call away from your employer's building and off its
network, and if a recruiter calls unexpectedly, return the call later from somewhere private
rather than answering it badly at your desk. For a video interview, test the link and the
camera before the day, and have the dial in number ready in case the video fails.

## Building real examples

### Pick a spread of real situations and know what happened, what you did, and what changed

Go back through your work history and pick a spread of situations that, between them, cover the
kinds of work the roles you are targeting actually involve. For each one, know what the
situation was, what you actually did in it, and what changed as a result. Write these down in
your own words rather than as a script to recite, because a recited answer sounds recited, and
an interviewer who has heard a hundred of them can tell.

### Say what you would do differently, because most people skip it

For each example, add one more piece: what you would do differently if you faced the same
situation now. This is the part almost everyone leaves out, and it is also the part that most
often makes an example land, because it shows judgment rather than just a result. An answer
that ends at what happened is a report. An answer that includes what you would change is a
reflection, and it is the reflection an interviewer remembers.

## Honesty under questioning

### Expect every claim to be tested, never invent a number, and say what you do not know

This is the same rule that runs through the rest of this manual, restated for the interview.
Every claim on your resume is a question waiting to be asked by someone who does the actual
work and will notice if the answer does not hold up. Never invent a number under pressure,
whether it is a team size, a dollar figure, or a timeline. If you do not know something, say so
plainly and say what you would do to find out. That answer is stronger than a bluff, and most
competent interviewers treat it as such.

## The stages and what each is for

### A recruiter screen confirms the basics, the range, and the timeline

A recruiter screen is usually short and exists to confirm the basics: what the role actually is,
the compensation range, your timeline, and whether the conversation is worth the hiring
manager's time. It is rarely where the job is won, but it is where a mismatch ends things early.

### A hiring manager conversation tests whether you can do the job and want to work together

This conversation is about substance and fit at once. The hiring manager is trying to work out
whether you can actually do the job and whether they want to work with you day to day. Bring
your real examples here rather than a generic pitch.

### A panel or technical stage tests specifics with the people who would be your peers

A panel or technical stage goes deeper into specifics, usually with people who would be your
future peers rather than your manager. Expect more detailed follow up questions and be ready to
say what you do not know rather than guess in front of people who would notice a guess.

### A final stage is usually about level and fit, so ask what to expect beforehand

By the final stage, a decision has often mostly been made, and the conversation is checking
level, fit, and confirming that decision rather than starting over. This sequence varies by
employer, so ask the recruiter early what the stages look like for this particular process.

## Questions to ask

### Ask what success in this role looks like in the first months

This tells you what the employer actually expects, which is often different from what the
posting says, and gives you something concrete to measure the offer against later.

### Ask what the biggest problem the person in this role needs to solve is

This surfaces the real priority behind the job, as opposed to the list of duties in the posting,
and often reveals whether the role is set up to succeed or set up to struggle.

### Ask why the role is open

The answer tells you whether this is growth, a replacement, or a role that has turned over more
than once, and each of those changes what you are walking into.

### Ask who you would work with most closely

This tells you about the working relationships that will shape your daily experience far more
than the org chart does.

### Ask how the team makes decisions

The answer tells you how much autonomy you would actually have and how disagreements get
resolved, both of which matter more once you are inside the job than they seem from outside it.

### Bring your questions regardless, because having none reads badly

Showing up with no questions reads as a lack of interest, and the underlying reason is simple,
an interview is also you deciding whether you want the job, not only them deciding about you.

## Talking about money and why you are leaving

### Treat the compensation conversation as a judgment call, and check what applies locally

Rules on what an employer may ask about pay history, or must publish as a range, vary by place
and change over time, so check what applies where you are rather than following one fixed rule
here. When the conversation comes up, treat it as a real judgment call rather than a script.

Bring a range you can explain rather than a single number pulled from memory or hope. A range
backed by research into what the role and level actually pay, with a reason behind it, is
stronger than a figure with nothing behind it either way.

### Answer the reason for leaving question once, plainly, the same way every time

Decide your answer once, state it plainly, and use the same words every time you are asked,
without editorializing on top of it. Consistency reads as confidence, and reworking the answer
each time invites follow up questions that do not need to exist.

### Name a layoff as a layoff, a fact about the employer rather than a verdict on you

If you left because of a layoff, say it was a layoff. That is a fact about the employer's
situation at the time, not a judgment about your performance, and it should be stated that
plainly rather than softened or dressed up.

## After the interview

### Write your notes immediately, including the questions that landed badly

Do this while the interview is still fresh, and put the notes into your tracker rather than
your memory. Include what went well and what did not, especially any question that landed
badly, since that is exactly what you will want to fix before the next one.

### Send a short specific thank you note, and follow up once on the timeline they gave you

A short, specific thank you note is ordinary advice, worth sending, and unlikely to be the
deciding factor either way. Beyond that, follow up once, on the timeline the interviewer or
recruiter actually gave you, rather than repeatedly before it has passed.

### Debrief yourself and fix the example that did not work before the next interview

This is the highest value thing you can do in the days after an interview. Look honestly at
which example fell flat or which question caught you off guard, and fix it before you walk into
the next one, rather than carrying the same weak spot forward untouched.

### Keep applying while a promising process runs, and ask once for feedback after a late rejection

A process that looks certain still ends without an offer often enough that pausing your search
for it is a real risk, so keep applying and keep interviewing elsewhere while it plays out. If
you are rejected at a late stage, ask once for feedback, and expect that you may not get any,
since many employers will not give it for reasons that have nothing to do with you.
